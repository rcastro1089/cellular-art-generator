Seeds (B2/S)

Every cell that's alive right now will be dead next generation, guaranteed — and yet Seeds is one of the most visually explosive rules in the Life-like family. It's proof that "nothing survives" doesn't mean "nothing happens."

Rule
B2/S
Birth condition
Exactly 2 neighbors
Survival condition
None — ever
Character
Explosive, lace-like
Neighborhood
Moore (8-cell)

How a Rule With No Survival Still Grows

In Conway's Game of Life, a cell needs 2 or 3 living neighbors to survive — Seeds removes survival from the equation entirely. Only birth matters: any dead cell with exactly 2 living neighbors comes alive next generation. Since every currently-living cell is guaranteed to die, the entire population is replaced every single tick — but replacement isn't the same as disappearance. As long as the previous generation's live cells create new birth conditions somewhere nearby, the pattern keeps regenerating, just never in the same place twice. The result looks less like a static structure evolving and more like a wave of activity rippling outward.

Why It Looks the Way It Does

Because nothing ever stabilizes into a still life or a clean oscillator (there's no "surviving" state to settle into), Seeds almost never produces the calm, structured look Conway's Life eventually reaches. Instead, a small seed pattern tends to expand outward in branching, lace-like fronts — visually closer to a chemical reaction-diffusion pattern or frost crystallizing than to a "living" simulation. It's one of the more strikingly different Life-like rules to render with long-exposure history mapping, since the trail left behind traces the entire expanding wavefront rather than a handful of persistent shapes.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does the Seeds rule B2/S mean?

A dead cell with exactly 2 living neighbors is born (B2); no living cell ever survives to the next generation, no matter how many neighbors it has (S is empty). Every currently-alive cell dies every single tick.

How can a rule where nothing survives produce interesting patterns?

Because births still happen based on the previous generation's live cells, new cells keep appearing even though none of the old ones remain. The pattern the births trace out is entirely different from the pattern that caused them, which produces a constantly shifting, lace-like structure rather than the static or oscillating shapes typical of Life-like rules with survival.

Is Seeds related to Conway's Game of Life?

Yes — it's a different point in the same Life-like rule family (2 states, Moore neighborhood, B/S notation), just with a different birth/survival combination. Removing all survival conditions turns out to produce a dramatically different, far more explosive personality than Conway's balanced B3/S23.
