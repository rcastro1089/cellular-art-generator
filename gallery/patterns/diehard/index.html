Diehard

Seven cells that grow, twist, and fragment for 130 generations — and then die completely, all at once, leaving an entirely empty grid behind. Diehard is one of the most dramatic small patterns in Conway's Game of Life.

A Pattern That Actually Dies

Most well-known methuselahs — like the R-pentomino — eventually settle into a stable mix of still lifes, oscillators, and drifting spaceships that persist forever. Diehard is different: it takes just as long to play out, growing and fragmenting for well over a hundred generations, but the ending is total. On exactly generation 130, every remaining living cell dies simultaneously under Conway's rule, and nothing is left — no still life, no oscillator, no glider drifting away. It's one of the more striking demonstrations that Conway's Game of Life doesn't guarantee eternal structure just because a pattern manages to survive for a while.

Why It Matters

Diehard is frequently cited alongside the Acorn and R-pentomino as one of the classic small methuselahs used to illustrate how unpredictable Conway's simple rule can be — but it earns its own distinct place in that group precisely because of its ending. Where most long-lived small patterns are notable for what they eventually produce, Diehard is notable for producing nothing at all, despite taking just as long to get there.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many generations does Diehard survive?

Exactly 130 generations. On generation 130, every living cell in the pattern dies simultaneously, leaving a completely empty grid with no survivors, no still lifes, and no spaceships left behind.

Why is it called Diehard?

The name refers to how long the pattern holds on before dying — it survives far longer than its 7-cell size would suggest, growing to dozens of live cells at points, before eventually collapsing to nothing.

Is Diehard the longest-lived pattern that fully dies out?

Among small, well-known starting patterns, Diehard is one of the most famous examples of a pattern that eventually reaches total extinction rather than stabilizing into still lifes or oscillators — it's specifically notable for how long it takes to die completely, not for surviving forever.
